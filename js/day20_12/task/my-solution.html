My answer is to build where the ground is solid

If page two is right and a city cannot depend on one industry, then my personal plan should not depend on one industry either. I studied Electrical and Electronics Engineering. I am learning software on my own. The field where both of those matter at the same time, and which nobody can outsource to a chatbot, is space technology. That is where I am aiming.

Why space, and not another software job

Four reasons, and none of them are about the job being glamorous.

⚡

My degree is actually useful there

Satellites run on power systems, control circuits, signals and embedded electronics. That is EEE. In a normal web job my degree is just a piece of paper. Here it is the qualification.

🏛️

It does not depend on one company surviving

Government space work continues through recessions and layoffs because it is national infrastructure, not a product that has to sell this quarter.

🔬

The work cannot be fully automated

You cannot let a tool generate the control software for a launch vehicle and hope for the best. Hardware that flies needs humans who are accountable for every decision.

🇮🇳

India is expanding here, not shrinking

Space is one of the few areas where the country is adding capability rather than cutting it, and private space startups are growing alongside government missions.

My roadmap

This is a real plan with real dates, not a wish.

Stage 1

Finish the software side

Complete backend with Java and Spring Boot, connect it to the React frontend I already know, and put two working projects online. Software is not my destination, but it is a skill I refuse to leave half-done.

Stage 2

Prepare for government technical exams

ISRO scientist/engineer recruitment, and similar technical posts through organisations that hire EEE graduates. These need core electrical subjects, so I go back and study them properly instead of forgetting them.

Stage 3

Build the overlap on purpose

Learn embedded systems, microcontrollers and basic control theory alongside programming. The rare person is not the coder or the electrical engineer — it is the one who is both.

Stage 4

Enter through whichever door opens first

Government space organisation, a private space or defence startup, or an electronics/embedded company. Any of these puts me on the path. I do not need the perfect entry — I need to get inside the field.

What I think should happen beyond just me

Page two showed the problem is a city standing on one pillar. These are the other pillars.

Right now colleges train students to clear one aptitude test and join one kind of company. If that one kind of company stops hiring, the whole batch is stuck. Teaching core engineering properly means a student has more than one direction to walk in.

Chennai already has an automobile and electronics manufacturing base. Growing that — electric vehicles, batteries, electronic components, defence manufacturing — creates jobs that do not vanish when a software contract ends, and it uses exactly the skills EEE and mechanical students already have.

Solar, wind and grid modernisation need electrical engineers permanently. Power cannot be imported as a service from another country the way software can. This is one of the safest long-term fields for someone with my degree.

My honest advice to my own classmates: learn to code, but attach it to something physical. Software plus agriculture, software plus medical devices, software plus energy, software plus space. The pure coding half is the part that automation eats first.

What I would do if IT disappeared tomorrow

I would not sit and wait for it to come back. My degree is in electrical and electronics engineering, and that field existed long before software did. Power systems, motors, circuits, transmission — those keep running whether or not anyone is writing web applications.

So the action I would take is simple: go deeper into the physical side. Prepare seriously for technical government exams, get into space or energy or manufacturing, and keep programming as a supporting skill rather than the only one I own. If software recovers, I have it. If it does not, I am not standing on it.

I am not choosing space because I am afraid IT will end. I am choosing it because it is the one place where everything I have studied so far finally adds up to one thing.

That is the whole argument across these three pages. IT is changing, not dying. If it did die, it would take far more than IT down with it. And the way to be safe in either case is to stop being only one thing.
