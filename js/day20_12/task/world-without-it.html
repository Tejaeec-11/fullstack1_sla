If developers had no jobs, who else would fall with them?

This is the part most people get wrong. They think if IT jobs go, only IT people suffer. But a developer's salary does not stay in the developer's pocket. It moves through the whole city. Follow the money and you find out that the mall, the cinema, the auto driver and the tea shop are all standing on the same floor.

The chain reaction

Press the button and watch what happens step by step when development work stops.

1
Development work stops

No new software is built. Companies stop hiring developers, testers, designers and support staff.

2
Salaries disappear from the city

In Chennai alone, lakhs of people working in OMR, Sholinganallur and Guindy stop earning. That is a huge amount of money that used to circulate every month.

3
Spending drops immediately

People stop going to malls, restaurants and cinemas. Not because they do not want to — because they are saving whatever is left.

4
Businesses lose their customers

The shop owner in the mall did not lose an IT job. He lost the people who used to walk into his shop. His sales fall even though he did nothing wrong.

5
Non-IT jobs start disappearing too

Shops close, so salespeople, security guards, housekeeping staff, cooks, delivery riders and auto drivers lose work. This is the part people forget.

6
Rent, property and everything else follows

Flats near IT corridors empty out. Landlords who took loans cannot repay. PG owners, tiffin centres and hostels shut down.

7
Savings run out

Most people can survive two or three years on their own money if they are careful. After that the question becomes serious: where do you go to earn, when the jobs that supported the other jobs are gone?

What this looks like in Chennai

Click each one to open it.

The whole stretch from Perungudi to Siruseri was built around software companies. Remove the companies and you are left with glass buildings, empty parking and roads nobody needs. The tea shops and mess outside every gate close within weeks, because their entire customer base was the people walking out at lunch time.

Weekend crowds at malls are mostly salaried people. The shop owner inside pays heavy rent expecting that crowd. When the crowd stops, he cannot pay rent, and his staff — who were never in IT — lose their jobs. Cinema tickets, parking attendants, food courts, all of it runs on the same assumption that people have spare money on a Saturday.

These are the people with the least cushion. A driver on the OMR route or a rider delivering food to an office block is earning directly from IT salaries without ever writing a line of code. When offices empty out, their income does not reduce slowly — it stops.

Thousands of people move to Chennai from other districts and states for IT work. They pay rent, they eat outside, they buy things. If they go back to their hometowns, the landlord with a housing loan and the PG owner with a mess contract are both stuck with bills and nobody to pay them.

Engineering colleges exist partly because placement exists. If campus drives stop, admissions drop, and colleges cut staff. Coaching centres and training institutes that sell "100% placement" have nothing left to sell. That affects lecturers and trainers who were never in a software company at all.

The point I am trying to make

Business people think they are independent of developers. A shop owner in a mall would say his work has nothing to do with software. But his customers are the developers. If developers have no income, the entrepreneur has no buyer, and both of them are out of work at the same time.

That is the real answer to "what if there is no IT". It is not that IT people suffer. It is that IT salaries are the fuel for a very large number of non-IT jobs in a city like Chennai, and when you remove the fuel, the non-IT jobs go out too. One group cannot survive without the other.

A developer losing a job is one problem. A city losing the money that developer used to spend is a much bigger one.

So does this mean IT will never end?

No. It means we should not build a country where one industry holds up everything else. If everything depends on one pillar, one crack brings the whole building down. The safer answer is more pillars — manufacturing, energy, agriculture technology, defence, healthcare, space. That is what the last page is about.
